How to hire a software developer for your idea (with a one-page brief)

By Pulseni · Updated 9 October 2026 · 3 min read

A good brief saves weeks of back-and-forth and gets you a more accurate price. It doesn't need to be technical, and it fits on one page. Here's what to put in it, what to leave out, and a template you can copy.

Why the brief matters

Developers price what they can picture. A vague brief gets you a vague quote, usually with a big safety margin added. A clear brief about the problem gets you a sharper price, fewer surprises and a product that does the right job.

The good news: you're the expert on the problem, and that's what a brief is mostly about. You don't need to know anything about code.

What goes in

  • The problem. What goes wrong today, in two or three sentences. Be specific: "Reports take four evenings a week" is better than "Reporting is inefficient".
  • Who has it. The person who feels the pain, and roughly how many people like them there are.
  • How it's done now. The spreadsheets, paper forms, WhatsApp groups or software people use today, and where it breaks.
  • The one job. The single thing the first version must do well.
  • Must-haves. A short list of what it can't work without.
  • Who uses it, and where. In an office? On site with a poor signal? On a phone with gloves on? This changes a lot.
  • What success looks like. How you'll know it's working, in plain terms: time saved, fewer mistakes, people paying.
  • Budget and timing. A range is fine. It helps the developer suggest what fits rather than guess.
  • What you already have. Sketches, example reports, a list of interested customers, a name, a logo.
  • Rules to follow. Anything about data, health, finance or your industry's regulations.

What to leave out

  • Which technology to use. Leave that to the developer, and ask them to explain their choice in plain English.
  • Every feature you can imagine. Put the extras in a "later" list so they're not lost, but keep them out of the first version.
  • Pixel-perfect designs. Rough sketches of the key screens help. Polished mock-ups usually get redone anyway.

A one-page template

Copy this into a document and fill it in. Short answers are fine.

Project brief

  • The problem: …
  • Who has it, and how many: …
  • How it's done today: …
  • The one job the first version must do: …
  • Must-haves: …
  • Later (not in the first version): …
  • Who uses it, on what device, where: …
  • How we'll know it's working: …
  • Budget range and ideal launch date: …
  • What I already have: …
  • Rules or regulations to follow: …

A filled-in example

Example · Check-in reports for a letting agent

  • The problem: check-in and check-out reports take three hours each to write up, and disputes over deposits drag on because photos aren't linked to rooms.
  • Who has it: inventory clerks and small letting agents. There are thousands across the UK.
  • How it's done today: photos on a phone, notes on paper, typed into a Word template in the evening.
  • The one job: walk the property room by room on a phone and have the report ready to send when you leave.
  • Must-haves: rooms and items checklist, photos attached to each item, condition ratings, tenant signature, PDF report.
  • Later: comparing check-in with check-out automatically, a landlord portal, links to letting software.
  • Who uses it: one clerk, on a phone, in properties that often have no signal.
  • Success: reports done on site, in under an hour.
  • Budget and timing: £15,000 to £25,000, live before the summer moving season.

Questions to ask the developer back

  • What would you leave out of the first version, and why?
  • What's the fixed price, and what exactly does it include?
  • When will I see something working?
  • Who owns the code and the product when it's done?
  • What will it cost to run each month, and to maintain?

A good developer will push back on parts of your brief. That's a good sign: it means they're thinking about your problem, not just the list.
